## The problem this site exists to solve

Poverty in Scotland is measured carefully and published openly. Almost none of
it reaches the people it describes.

The figures sit in spreadsheets, in PDFs, and behind words like "reserved",
"devolved", "equivalised" and "after housing costs". To find out what is
happening in your own town you need to know which dataset to open, which year is
current, and how to read it.

There is a second barrier that matters more. Most people do not know who their
MP is, who their MSP is, or which of the two decides the thing they are angry
about. Not knowing is the main reason people do nothing. It is not apathy. The
whole business is arranged so that it feels like it is meant for somebody else.

## What Scotland Counted is

A free, independent guide to poverty, work and living costs in Scotland.

It takes official figures, explains them in ordinary words, shows what they mean
for your own area, and then makes it easy to put a question to the person who
can actually answer it.

It is a personal project. No political party, no charity, no campaign, no
funding, no paywall, no advertising, no sign-up.

## The scale of what it covers

- All 32 Scottish council areas, each with its own page
- All 57 Scottish MP constituencies, each with its own page
- Six measures tracked over time: child poverty, work, out-of-work benefits,
  pay, neighbourhoods and life expectancy
- Plain-English explainers on the things people actually search for

## The picture it shows

- About 17% of people in Scotland live in relative poverty after housing costs.
  That is roughly 940,000 people. About one in six.
- Around 21% of children, 18% of working-age adults and 13% of pensioners.
- Glasgow has the worst child poverty rate in Scotland at 36.1%, and it has
  risen faster there than anywhere else in the country.
- Scotland wrote four child poverty targets into law for 2023/24 and missed
  every one of them. Ministers have confirmed there is no penalty for missing
  them.

## Four things you can do on it

**1. See the figures for where you live.**
Enter a postcode, or pick your council area. You get the local child poverty
rate, how it compares with the rest of Scotland, whether it is rising or
falling, and the exact source underneath.

**2. Work out your own money.**
Two free calculators, both of which run entirely in your own browser. Nothing
you type is sent anywhere.

- The take-home pay calculator uses Scotland's six income tax bands, not the
  three used in the rest of the UK. Enter a wage and see what actually reaches
  your account, or work backwards from the pay you need to live on. It handles
  pensions, student loans and tax codes.
- The council tax calculator covers every band in all 32 councils, and it
  includes the water and waste water charges. Most published council tax
  figures leave those out, which is why other sites show you a smaller number.
  At Band A the water charge alone is about £435 a year.

**3. Understand who decides what.**
The UK Government sets Universal Credit and help with private rent. The Scottish
Government sets the Scottish Child Payment, housing and childcare. Councils run
homelessness services. Each can point at another and be partly right, which is
exactly why nothing gets fixed. The site explains the split in plain words and
shows how Scotland's MPs actually voted on the record.

**4. Email your MP and your MSP.**
This is the part the site is really for. Enter a postcode. It finds both people,
adds the poverty figures for your own area, writes both emails, and opens them
in your own email app. You read them and press send.

You have both an MP and an MSP. You do not need to have voted for them. Between
them they are paid over £176,000 a year, an MP £98,599, an MSP £77,711, and
answering people who live in their area is the job, not a favour.

## Why a wage is not the same as enough

Full-time work at the legal minimum of £12.71 an hour, 37.5 hours a week, comes
to £24,785 a year before tax. After Scottish income tax and National Insurance
that is about £21,404, or roughly £1,784 a month.

Average rent on a Glasgow two-bedroom flat is about £865 a month. Council tax,
water, energy and food come out of what is left. The site shows the whole
household budget rather than asserting the conclusion.

## Why you can trust the numbers

- Every figure links to the organisation that published it, ONS, DWP, the
  Scottish Government, End Child Poverty, named research.
- Where a figure has been worked out rather than published, the working is
  shown so anyone can check it.
- Where a number is commonly misread, the site says so. The often-quoted average
  Glasgow pay figure counts full-time employee jobs only, and it counts jobs in
  Glasgow rather than Glaswegians. The site explains that instead of repeating
  it.
- Corrections are published in public, with a dated log.
- All the underlying data is free to download and reuse.

## The one thing to remember

Poverty has an address, and so does power. The figures already exist. What is
missing is people asking the person responsible a question they have to answer
in writing.

It takes about a minute.

> Make a two-minute explainer introducing a website called Scotland Counted, for
> an audience in Scotland who have never followed politics and would not know
> what an MSP does. Warm, direct, no jargon, no hype and no sales language.
> Short sentences. Second person, "you", not "users".
>
> Structure it as: the problem (the figures exist but never reach the people
> they describe, and nobody knows who to ask), what the site is, the four things
> you can do on it, why the numbers can be trusted, and finish on the single
> action, enter your postcode, read the email, press send, it takes about a
> minute.
>
> Do not read out long lists of statistics. Use at most four numbers in the
> whole video and let them land. Never imply the site is run by a charity,
